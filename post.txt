**The Bewitched Man — Francisco Goya’s Haunting Vision of Superstition, Fear, and the Supernatural 🕯️🎨**

What happens when a person becomes so convinced that he is cursed that an ordinary object turns into the difference between life and death?

Painted around **1798 by Francisco de Goya (1746–1828)**, *The Bewitched Man* (*El hechizado por fuerza*), also known as *The Devil’s Lamp*, is a fascinating work that explores superstition, psychological terror, and the power of belief.

Unlike Goya’s more familiar depictions of witches and supernatural creatures, this painting draws its disturbing atmosphere from a theatrical story in which fear itself becomes a form of imprisonment.

**The Story Behind the Painting 🕯️**

The painting illustrates a scene from *El hechizado por fuerza* (*The Man Bewitched by Force*), a theatrical work by the Spanish playwright **Antonio de Zamora**.

The protagonist, Don Claudio, believes that he has been bewitched. According to the story, his life depends on keeping a lamp burning. If the flame goes out, he fears that a terrifying supernatural consequence will follow.

Goya captures this moment of anxious dependence, transforming a theatrical scene into a striking visual exploration of fear and irrational belief.

The lamp is not merely a source of light. Within the story, it becomes a symbol of survival, while the darkness surrounding it suggests the frightening power of the unknown.

**Fear, Superstition, and the Human Mind**

One of the most compelling aspects of this painting is the relationship between reality and imagination. Don Claudio's terror arises from his belief that supernatural forces control his fate.

Goya presents superstition not simply as a belief in magic, but as something capable of controlling human behavior. When a person becomes convinced that danger is unavoidable, even the smallest action can become filled with extraordinary significance.

The scene also reflects a broader concern that appears throughout Goya's work: the ways ignorance, irrationality, and fear can distort human judgment.

**A Painting Created for an Unusual Collection**

*The Bewitched Man* was one of six paintings involving witches and devils that Goya produced for the **Duke and Duchess of Osuna**, influential Spanish aristocratic patrons who maintained an estate at Alameda de Osuna near Madrid.

During this period, Goya explored themes of witchcraft, superstition, and the supernatural in works that combined theatrical imagination with social observation. These paintings reflect the fascination with supernatural subjects that existed alongside the intellectual debates of the late eighteenth century.

Goya's treatment of such themes was often ambiguous: he could evoke the supernatural while also encouraging viewers to question the fears and beliefs that gave it power.

**From Spanish Theatre to London’s National Gallery**

Today, the painting is part of the collection of the **National Gallery in London, United Kingdom**, where it is also catalogued under the title *A Scene from 'The Forcibly Bewitched'*.

Its connection to a theatrical comedy makes it especially interesting. Rather than presenting a straightforward horror scene, Goya transforms a stage narrative into a psychological image, allowing viewers to consider both its dramatic humor and its unsettling atmosphere.

**Artwork Details**

• **Artist:** Francisco de Goya (1746–1828)  
• **Title:** *The Bewitched Man*  
• **Alternative title:** *The Devil’s Lamp*  
• **Spanish title:** *El hechizado por fuerza*  
• **Date:** Approximately 1798  
• **Medium:** Oil on canvas  
• **Dimensions:** 42.5 × 30.8 cm  
• **Subject:** Don Claudio, a man who believes himself to be bewitched  
• **Literary source:** A theatrical work by Antonio de Zamora  
• **Artistic context:** Spanish art of the late eighteenth century  
• **Current location:** National Gallery, London, United Kingdom  

**Why This Painting Still Matters**

Goya's *The Bewitched Man* reminds us that fear does not always require a visible monster. Sometimes, the most powerful source of terror is the conviction that something terrible will happen if we fail to follow a particular rule.

Through a seemingly simple scene involving a man and a lamp, Goya explores the fragile boundary between belief and reality, revealing how superstition can transform everyday life into a psychological prison.

**Perhaps the most frightening force in this painting is not the darkness itself, but the fear of what might happen when the light disappears.**

**Saturn Devouring His Son — Francisco Goya’s Terrifying Vision of Fear, Power, and Destruction 🎨🖤**

Created between approximately **1820 and 1823** by the Spanish artist **Francisco Goya (1746–1828)**, *Saturn Devouring His Son* (*Saturno devorando a su hijo*) is one of the most disturbing and psychologically powerful paintings in Western art history.

The painting depicts a monstrous figure, traditionally identified as Saturn, the Roman equivalent of the Greek god Cronus, devouring the body of one of his children. With bulging eyes, an agitated expression, and hands gripping the mutilated body, the figure conveys a horrifying mixture of fear, desperation, and uncontrollable violence.

Its dark background, rough brushwork, and stark contrasts between light and shadow intensify the scene, making it feel less like a distant mythological episode and more like a nightmare brought to life.

**The Myth Behind the Horror ⚡**

According to Greek mythology, Cronus learned of a prophecy that one of his children would overthrow him, just as he had overthrown his own father, Uranus. Terrified of losing his power, Cronus swallowed each of his children at birth.

However, his wife, Rhea, saved their youngest son, Zeus, by hiding him and giving Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes instead. Zeus eventually returned, freed his siblings, and fulfilled the prophecy by overthrowing his father.

Goya's painting draws upon this ancient myth, transforming the story into an intensely visceral image of a ruler consumed by the fear of being replaced.

**A Masterpiece Born from Darkness**

Unlike many of Goya's earlier works, which included portraits, court paintings, and scenes of everyday life, *Saturn Devouring His Son* belongs to a deeply personal group of works known as the **Black Paintings** (*Pinturas negras*).

Goya created these paintings directly on the walls of his house, the Quinta del Sordo, near Madrid, during the final years of his life in Spain. Their disturbing imagery, dark palette, and ambiguous meanings distinguish them from conventional decorative painting.

The exact meaning of *Saturn Devouring His Son* remains uncertain. Interpretations have connected it to the destructive nature of power, the fear of aging, the passage of time, violence, and the consuming force of human obsession. Some readings also relate the Black Paintings to Goya's personal circumstances and the turbulent political climate of Spain. However, no single interpretation has been conclusively established.

**From a Private Wall to a Public Museum**

Goya did not create the Black Paintings as a conventional public exhibition. After his death, the paintings remained associated with the Quinta del Sordo. In the nineteenth century, they were transferred from the house's walls onto canvas, allowing them to be preserved and displayed independently.

Today, *Saturn Devouring His Son* is held by the **Museo Nacional del Prado in Madrid, Spain**, where it remains one of Goya's most famous and unsettling works.

**Artwork Details**

• **Artist:** Francisco Goya (1746–1828)  
• **Title:** *Saturn Devouring His Son*  
• **Spanish title:** *Saturno devorando a su hijo*  
• **Date:** Approximately 1820–1823  
• **Series:** The Black Paintings (*Pinturas negras*)  
• **Medium:** Originally painted as a mural on the walls of Goya's house; subsequently transferred to canvas  
• **Dimensions:** Approximately 146 × 83 cm  
• **Artistic period:** Romanticism  
• **Subject:** Saturn, associated with the Greek myth of Cronus  
• **Current location:** Museo del Prado, Madrid, Spain  
• **Accession number:** P000763  

**Why Does This Painting Still Haunt Us?**

Goya's masterpiece goes beyond illustrating an ancient myth. It presents a vision of destruction driven by fear: a figure so desperate to preserve his power that he destroys the very future he seeks to control.

Its unsettling imagery has influenced how generations of viewers understand horror, psychological expression, and the darker possibilities of art.

Perhaps its most disturbing message is this: **the fear of losing power can become so consuming that it destroys everything—including the very things that give power meaning.**

🌑 **WITCHES’ SABBATH: THE GREAT HE-GOAT — FRANCISCO GOYA (1820–1823)** 🎨

A gathering of fear, superstition, and darkness—captured in one of the most unsettling paintings in Western art.

🖼️ **The Artwork**

*Witches’ Sabbath*, also known as *The Great He-Goat* (*El gran cabrón*), is a mural painted by the Spanish artist Francisco de Goya between 1820 and 1823. It depicts a large, horned goat-like figure, commonly interpreted as Satan, presiding over a tightly packed gathering of witches.

The figure stands in dark silhouette against a muted, earthy background. Around it, a crowd of mostly elderly women watches with expressions of fear, fascination, and unease. A pale, veiled figure sits in the foreground, creating a striking contrast with the surrounding darkness.

Goya transforms a traditional supernatural subject into something deeply disturbing. The painting evokes themes of intimidation, ageing, death, and the power of superstition.

📜 **A Painting Born in Isolation**

Goya created this work during the final years of his life, when he was approximately 75 years old. He was living in a country house outside Madrid known as the *Quinta del Sordo*—literally, the “House of the Deaf Man.”

The painting belongs to a group of fourteen works now known as the **Black Paintings**. Goya painted these directly onto the plaster walls of his home, using dark colours and unsettling imagery. He left no known explanation of their intended meanings or original titles.

Some art historians interpret *Witches’ Sabbath* as a criticism of superstition, irrational belief, and the culture of fear associated with witchcraft accusations and religious persecution in Spain. However, Goya’s precise intentions remain uncertain.

🖌️ **The Lost Edges of the Original**

One remarkable fact about this painting is that it was originally much wider than the version we see today.

Around 1874, decades after Goya’s death, the mural was removed from the walls of his house and transferred to canvas. During this process, approximately 140 centimetres were cut from its right-hand side, significantly changing the composition and its visual balance.

Today, the painting is preserved at the **Museo del Prado in Madrid, Spain**.

📌 **ARTWORK DETAILS**

• **Artist:** Francisco de Goya (1746–1828)
• **Date:** 1820–1823
• **Title:** *Witches’ Sabbath* / *The Great He-Goat*
• **Original medium:** Oil on plaster wall
• **Current support:** Canvas
• **Dimensions:** Approximately 140.5 × 435.7 cm
• **Artistic series:** The Black Paintings
• **Location:** Museo del Prado, Madrid, Spain

💭 **Why Does It Still Matter?**

Goya’s painting is more than a frightening supernatural scene. It invites us to question how fear can shape collective beliefs, how superstition can influence society, and how darkness can become a powerful artistic language.

The horned figure dominates the crowd, but the expressions of the surrounding people may be even more revealing. Their faces suggest a world in which fear, fascination, and unquestioned belief coexist.

Nearly two centuries later, the painting continues to challenge viewers—not because it explains everything, but because it leaves so much open to interpretation.

**“When fear becomes belief, even the shadows can command a crowd.”**

🌙 WITCHES’ SABBATH (1798) — FRANCISCO GOYA’S DARK VISION OF THE SUPERNATURAL 🎨

What happens when fear, superstition, and imagination come together in a single painting?

Created by Spanish artist Francisco Goya between 1797 and 1798, Witches’ Sabbath (El aquelarre) is a haunting oil painting that draws viewers into a mysterious world of witchcraft and supernatural imagery.

🖼️ WHAT DOES THE PAINTING SHOW?

At the center stands a large, dark, horned goat-like figure, surrounded by a group of people gathered in an isolated landscape beneath a night sky. Some figures appear to watch the central creature, while others sit or kneel around it.

A crescent moon hangs in the upper-left corner, and dark, bat-like shapes move across the sky, adding to the painting’s unsettling atmosphere.

The horned figure is commonly associated with the Devil in traditional European depictions of witches’ gatherings. The scene evokes a witches’ sabbath—a gathering traditionally imagined in folklore as a meeting between witches and supernatural forces.

Goya uses dramatic contrasts between darkness and light, expressive faces, and unusual figures to create a scene that feels mysterious, disturbing, and dreamlike.

🔍 WHO CREATED IT, AND WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?

Francisco Goya (1746–1828) was one of Spain’s most influential artists. His work explored a wide range of subjects, including human behavior, social criticism, superstition, violence, and the darker side of the imagination.

This painting belongs to a group of works associated with witchcraft that Goya produced during the late eighteenth century. Such imagery can be understood within the historical context of European beliefs about witchcraft and the fascination with the supernatural.

Although the painting presents a supernatural scene, it should be understood as an artistic depiction of witchcraft imagery—not as evidence that the events shown actually occurred.

📜 THE HISTORY BEHIND THE CANVAS

In 1798, the painting was purchased alongside five other works related to witchcraft by the Duke and Duchess of Osuna. It is not known for certain whether the couple commissioned these paintings or purchased them after Goya had completed them.

During the twentieth century, the painting was acquired by Spanish financier and art collector José Lázaro Galdiano. Following his death, it was donated to the Spanish state.

Today, the painting is held at the Museo Lázaro Galdiano in Madrid, Spain.

📌 ARTWORK DETAILS

🎨 Title: Witches’ Sabbath (El aquelarre)
👨‍🎨 Artist: Francisco Goya
📅 Date: 1797–1798
🖌️ Medium: Oil on canvas
📐 Dimensions: 43 × 30 cm (approximately 17 × 12 inches)
🏛️ Current location: Museo Lázaro Galdiano, Madrid, Spain

💭 WHY DOES THIS PAINTING STILL CAPTURE OUR ATTENTION?

Perhaps its greatest power lies in what it leaves unexplained. The figures, the horned creature, the moonlit sky, and the surrounding darkness invite us to consider how fear and superstition shaped the human imagination.

Goya does not present a comforting fantasy. Instead, he creates an unsettling visual world that encourages us to examine the relationship between belief, fear, and the unknown.

Nearly 230 years later, the painting remains a fascinating example of how art can transform the supernatural into something vivid, mysterious, and unforgettable.

Sometimes, a painting does more than show us a scene—it reveals the fears and beliefs of the world that imagined it. 🌑
